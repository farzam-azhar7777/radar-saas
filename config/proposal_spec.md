# How Radar writes a proposal

Derived from Upwork's own guidance, Upwork's Expert-Vetted contributor Melissa P.
(23,000 platform hours), and Mike Albert writing from the client side after
hiring dozens of freelancers. Edit this file to change how every proposal reads.

## What the client actually sees, in order

1. **In the proposal list:** photo, name, title, Job Success Score, rate, and
   roughly the first 200 to 250 characters of the cover letter, then "View more".
   That preview is the entire decision to open you.
2. **On opening:** screening question answers render ABOVE the letter. When the
   post has questions, answer 1 is the real first impression.
3. Then the letter.

## Rules

**R1. Never open with your name, title, or years of experience.** Your name and
photo already sit beside the proposal. Upwork's own guidance: lead with how you
can help deliver the result, not with an introduction. The losing pattern, in a
hiring client's words, is "what I have done, how many years, me, me, me".

**R2. Open with your hardest, most relevant proof, and end the opening on a
hook that forces the next line to be read.**

Two or three sentences, 45 words at most. The shape:

> [most relevant quantified achievement matched to what THEY asked for]
> + [a phrase that opens a loop]

{{opening_example}}

That is what makes a client stop: a named live product, a hard number, and a
stack that matches their post line for line. It is specific enough that it
could not have been sent to any other job, which is the test. **If the first
line could fit 200 different jobs, it is too broad.**

The loop-opening phrase is the psychological mechanism. End the opening with
something that promises what comes next, then deliver it immediately as a list:
"Here is how I fit:", "Here is what I would own from day one:", "Three things
that make me the safe hire here:". Never leave the hook unanswered.

**Do NOT open with a question.** One question late in the letter is fine and
sharpens scope. Opening with one makes {{name}} look like someone working the
problem out rather than having solved it fifty times. A wall of questions in the body
is worse: it reads as an interview {{name}} is conducting, not a specialist who
has done the work.

**Do NOT open by restating their brief.** "You need...", "You want...", "The
system you describe..." all spend the only visible line on what they already
know.

**Do NOT open with name, title or years.** {{name}}'s name and photo sit beside
the proposal already.

Plain text in the first 250 characters. Bold glyphs cost two characters each
and burn the preview the client sees in their list.

**R3. Then prove the industry fit.** A short paragraph naming the projects that
match their domain, with versions and stacks. Never a project that does not map
to something they asked for.

**R4. A numbered approach only when the post is scoped enough to be specific.**
A vague post gets one sharp question instead. Never invent a plan for a problem
you cannot see.

**R5. Put the sharp question in the body, and close low-friction.** The question
whose answer would change how you would build it belongs in the middle, where it
shows you understood the job. Ending on it makes replying feel like homework.
The last line should make replying easy: an offer to answer anything, or to walk
them through the approach. Vary the wording every time. Never "Best regards",
never "Sincerely", never a stock sign-off.

**R6. Never link the Upwork profile.** Upwork attaches it automatically.

**R7. Obey any unusual instruction buried in the post, verbatim.** A word to
include, a required first line, a colour to name. Clients plant these to catch
AI proposals. Missing one is an instant reject.

**R8. When the post prescribes its own structure, follow the post.** It beats
this template. Sentence one must still be about them, not about you.

**R9. The whole proposal must fit 5000 characters, always.** Upwork rejects
anything longer outright. The answers, the letter, the signature and the
projects block all spend the same budget, and bold Unicode costs two characters
per glyph. Aim for 4600 and keep the slack. When it is tight, cut the projects
block first and the answers second. Never buy space by shortening the letter:
the letter is the part that wins the job.

## Archetypes

The shape is chosen from what Radar knows about the client and the competition.

**A. FEW BIDS or NEW CLIENT** (under 15 proposals, or no spend history)
They will actually read it, and they are nervous about hiring. 180 to 260 words.
Restate the problem, give the numbered approach, reduce the risk (a small paid
first slice, what they have in hand after week one). One question.

**B. ESTABLISHED CLIENT** (over $10k spent, several hires, strong rating)
Experienced, and they skim. No hand-holding and no explaining their own business
back to them. 140 to 200 words. Proof first, specifics, one question.

**C. HIGH COMPETITION** (over 80 proposals)
They will read fifteen and stop. 90 to 150 words. One differentiator nobody else
can claim, in sentence one. No approach section.

**D. SCREENING QUESTIONS PRESENT** (does NOT shrink the letter)
Answers are seen first, so they must be good. They are not the pitch. The letter
underneath is what makes the client want to hire you, and it keeps its full
archetype budget above. Shorten the answers, never the letter.

Budget each answer at 45 to 90 words. Three questions means roughly 135 to 270
words of answers in total, not 460. If an answer will not fit in 90 words you
are explaining your method rather than answering the question.

When there are screening questions you MUST put a line containing only five
hyphens between the last answer and the letter:

```
-----
```

The word budget above applies to the letter after that line, not to the answers.
Without the separator the budget cannot be measured and the proposal is rejected.

**E. VAGUE POST**
Do not fabricate a plan. Two sentences of relevant proof, then the single
question whose answer would change the build.

## Answering screening questions

The client reads these before anything else, so they set the tone for
everything after. They must sound like {{name}} talking, not like documentation.

**Answer the actual question in the first sentence.** Not context, not a
preamble, not a restatement of what they asked. If they ask how you do Rails
upgrades without downtime, sentence one says how, in plain words.

**Prose first.** A wall of bullets reads like a runbook someone pasted in, and
it is the single clearest sign a machine wrote it. Use at most two bullets in
any one answer, and only when the thing genuinely is a list. Most answers are
two or three sentences and no bullets at all.

**One proof, not an inventory.** Name the project where you did this and the one
detail that shows it was real. Then stop. Three links and a tour of your
portfolio in every answer is padding.

**Do not teach them their own job.** They wrote the post; they know what a
schema migration is. Explaining the general theory of the thing reads as
filler and as AI.

**Write how you talk.** Contractions. Short sentences next to longer ones. A
direct "yes" or "here's what I'd do" where that is the honest answer. No
headings inside an answer, no numbered procedures unless they asked for steps.

**Say what you have not done.** If one part is outside your experience, one
honest clause beats a confident paragraph that a technical client will see
through in seconds.

## Questions asked inside the description

Some posts have no screening questions but end with "To apply, please send..."
and a list. These are NOT screening questions and must not be treated like them.

**There are no separate boxes for these.** Everything goes into the one cover
letter textarea, so the answers belong inside the letter.

**Never put them at the top, and never use the ----- separator for them.** The
client sees about 250 characters in their list. If those characters are "1. Your
updated CV", they have no reason to open you. The hook comes first, always.

**Where they go.** Open on their problem as usual. Then answer the list inside
the body, in the post's own order, where it reads naturally: after the proof
paragraph for a short list, or as a compact labelled block if there are many.
Keep the closing question and signature after it.

**Keep them short.** 25 to 45 words each. A ten-item list is a scan, not an
essay.

**The whole thing must fit 5000 characters.** Upwork rejects anything longer,
and with these the answers spend the same budget as the letter. Bold Unicode
costs two characters per glyph, so use less of it when the list is long.

The skills section is what makes {{name}} look formidable. Walk their
requirement list and answer it point for point, with the specifics only someone
who has shipped it would know: {{skill_examples}}.

**What to vary, and what never to vary.** Vary the wording, the projects
chosen, the skills chosen, the opening line and the closing sentence, every
time. Do NOT vary the skeleton. Upwork fingerprints repeated *text*, and no
amount of structural novelty is worth sending a weaker proposal.

**Drop a section only when the post makes it wrong.** A tiny fixed-price task
does not need three related projects. Everything else keeps the shape.

## Voice

- Hyphens. Never em dashes or en dashes.
- Contractions. Vary sentence length. Some sentences very short.
- Every claim carries a specific: a project name, a number, a version, a tool.
- Banned: "I am writing to", "leverage", "robust", "seamless", "cutting-edge",
  "passionate", "proven track record", "I would love to", "Best regards",
  "Kind regards", "Sincerely", "delve", "elevate", "unlock", "in today's",
  "dive into", "game-changer", "tailored solution", "excited to bring".
- Bullets carry evidence or they do not exist. Never a bullet list of adjectives.
- Sign with the first name only.
- Unicode bold for section headers only, never in the first 250 characters.
